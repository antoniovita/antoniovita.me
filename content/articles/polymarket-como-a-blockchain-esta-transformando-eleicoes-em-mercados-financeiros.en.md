Over the past few years, prediction markets have gone from a curiosity of the crypto ecosystem to a new lens for reading politics, the economy and global events. Among the platforms leading this shift, Polymarket has become the prime example of how blockchain-based markets can turn uncertainty into a price.

Prediction markets are markets in which users trade contracts tied to the outcome of future events. Those events can include elections, court rulings, regulatory approvals, geopolitical conflicts, macroeconomic indicators or cultural happenings. Instead of simply following polls, headlines or expert analysis, users watch a price that reflects the implied probability the market assigns to a given outcome.

On Polymarket, each event is structured as a market. An election, for instance, is no longer just a political process; it also becomes a tradable asset: “Will candidate X win?”, “Will party Y win a majority?”, “Will a given result be certified by a specific date?”. Users buy positions on possible outcomes, and the price of those positions becomes a real-time gauge of collective expectations.

The model gained momentum especially during the 2024 U.S. election cycle. Polymarket’s “Presidential Election Winner 2024” market racked up $3.7 billion in trading volume, becoming one of the clearest examples of how prediction markets can draw scale, liquidity and public attention around high-stakes political events. (Polymarket)

Polymarket’s significance isn’t just about trading volume. It lies in the fact that it offers a new way of looking at political reality: not only through stated intentions, as in polls, nor only through narrative analysis, as in the media, but through prices set by participants who put capital at risk.

## Prediction markets as a new information infrastructure

The logic of a prediction market is simple. A market poses an objective question about a future event. Users can buy positions that pay out if a given outcome occurs. On Polymarket, outcome shares trade between $0.00 and $1.00, and the price reflects the market’s belief about the probability of that event happening. A “Yes” share trading at $0.65 signals, roughly, an implied probability of 65%. (Polymarket Documentation)

This mechanism turns real-world events into financial instruments. An election stops being merely a political event and is represented by contracts whose prices move as new information emerges. Polls, debates, scandals, court rulings, speeches and campaign moves can shift the market price almost instantly.

The key difference is the incentive. On social media, opinions can earn status, engagement or a sense of belonging. In prediction markets, wrong opinions cost money. That doesn’t eliminate bias, manipulation or irrationality, but it creates a structure in which users have an incentive to separate personal preference from forecasting.

The question is no longer just “Who would you vote for?” but “How much would you stake to prove you’re right?”

That shift is what makes Polymarket relevant as an electoral metric. It doesn’t merely measure stated intention. It measures conviction with a price tag.

## Why Polymarket became an electoral metric

Polymarket gained ground because it offers something that polls, pundits and traditional models can’t always deliver: a continuous, liquid metric updated in real time, thanks to the blockchain’s always-on nature.

Election polls are essential, but they are snapshots of a specific period with their own metrics. They depend on samples, methodology, weighting and margins of error. Statistical models turn those polls into probabilities, but they still depend on input data and on the model’s assumptions. Prediction markets, by contrast, aggregate the expectations of participants who have a financial incentive to correct prices they see as wrong.

For example, a poll might show Candidate A slightly ahead, but with a meaningful margin of error. On Polymarket, traders interpret that data point immediately: if they think the market has underestimated Candidate A, they buy that candidate’s contract. If they think the poll is weak, they buy the opposite side. That tug-of-war shows up in the price: if the contract rises from $0.48 to $0.56, the market is now pricing in roughly a 56% chance of that outcome.

The sector’s growth shows that demand for this kind of metric has risen quickly. According to data reported by MarketWatch, monthly volume across the leading prediction markets, including Polymarket and Kalshi, went from about $5 billion at the end of 2024 to $24 billion in March 2026. (MarketWatch)

That rise suggests prediction markets are no longer just a tool for election speculation. Investors, strategists and financial institutions now also watch them as instruments for gauging expectations about macroeconomic events, Federal Reserve decisions, political risk and other high-stakes binary scenarios.

## Regulatory risks and insider trading

The growth of prediction markets has also drawn more regulatory scrutiny. In the United States, Polymarket has already been targeted by the CFTC, which in 2022 ordered it to pay a $1.4 million penalty and required it to wind down markets that did not comply with applicable law. The case showed that the challenge isn’t only technological: depending on the country and the regulatory framework, prediction markets can be treated as event contracts, derivatives, binary options or gambling.

In Brazil, the debate has moved forward as well. In April 2026, the government blocked access to platforms such as Polymarket and Kalshi as part of a crackdown on prediction markets deemed similar to unauthorized betting. According to Reuters, the Conselho Monetário Nacional (National Monetary Council) restricted derivatives tied to sporting, political, electoral, cultural and social events, allowing only contracts linked to economic and financial indicators, such as interest rates, exchange rates and price indexes. (Reuters)

Beyond regulation, there is the risk of trading on inside information. Prediction markets work best when they attract participants with superior information, but that becomes a problem when the information is confidential or obtained through an institutional position. This is one of the model’s central paradoxes: the more efficient the market is at aggregating information, the greater the risk of attracting insiders.

The regulatory question, then, is how to let these markets reveal collective expectations without turning privileged information into a financial opportunity, or creating a new layer of gambling dressed up as a financial product.

## Conclusion

Polymarket has become relevant because it turns real-world events into liquid markets updated in real time. In elections, this creates a new political metric: the probability assigned by people who put capital at risk.

Its potential lies in turning scattered information into a public signal. But its risks are just as clear: manipulation, insider trading and regulatory clashes, as the block in Brazil and the scrutiny in the United States show.

In the end, the big question is whether prediction markets are merely measuring reality or starting to shape it.
