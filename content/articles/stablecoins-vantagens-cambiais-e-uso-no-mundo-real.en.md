## Recent changes and their impact

Over the past few years, stablecoins have established themselves as one of the most important pieces of financial infrastructure in the crypto ecosystem. Unlike volatile cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin or Ethereum, stablecoins are digital assets designed to hold parity with fiat currencies, above all the U.S. dollar.

That stability allows them to serve not only as trading instruments within the crypto market but also as digital monetary infrastructure for payments, remittances and storing value, something that historically wasn't possible in crypto because of the extreme volatility of the first crypto assets.

Today, stablecoins move trillions of dollars in annual volume and already account for a significant share of global on-chain activity. According to data from Chainalysis (2023) and CoinMetrics (2024), stablecoins frequently outpace other cryptocurrencies in transaction volume, and they are used by individuals as well as by companies and institutions.

At the same time, adoption has grown especially fast in emerging economies, where structural factors make the traditional financial system less efficient. Chief among them are:

- high inflation
- capital controls
- high banking costs
- difficulty accessing dollars

Against this backdrop, stablecoins have become a form of digital access to the dollar, allowing individuals and businesses to move capital around the world at lower cost and with near-instant settlement, since blockchain networks never stop running.

## Frictions in International Foreign Exchange

Despite the growing digitization of the global financial system, the international foreign exchange market is still marked by heavy intermediation, high costs and operational limitations. Much of this infrastructure was built before the modern internet and still depends on traditional banking networks, even as a recent wave of fintechs has set out to reduce these inefficiencies.

International transfers, for instance, rarely move directly between two financial institutions. Instead, they typically pass through a chain of correspondent banks that intermediate the settlement of the transaction.

This model, widely used across the international financial system, creates a number of structural inefficiencies.

### High costs of international remittances

One of the main problems is the cost of international transfers. According to the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide report (2023), the global average cost of sending international remittances hovers around 6% of the amount transferred, and can exceed 8% on certain corridors between emerging economies.

These costs are made up of several components:

- fees charged by financial institutions
- FX spreads applied to the conversion rate
- intermediation fees charged by correspondent banks
- operating costs of remittance providers

For small transfers, especially those sent by migrant workers, these costs can take a significant bite out of the income sent to families abroad.

### Long settlement times

Beyond cost, international payments often come with relatively long settlement times. In many cases, transfers can take one to three business days to complete.

The delay stems from several factors:

- reconciliation between correspondent banks
- time zone differences across jurisdictions
- limited banking hours
- compliance and regulatory verification processes

Even in a highly digitized financial system, these steps remain necessary within traditional banking infrastructure.

By contrast, blockchain-based networks operate 24 hours a day, seven days a week, enabling near-instant settlement of transactions, in minutes or even seconds, depending on the network used.

### Capital controls and FX restrictions

Another important feature of the international FX system is the presence of capital controls in many economies. Governments often use these mechanisms to manage currency flows and protect international reserves.

Although they play an important role in macroeconomic stability, such measures can also restrict individuals' and businesses' access to foreign currencies.

A number of countries have put measures of this kind in place at different times, including:

- Argentina — for years it maintained strict currency controls, known as the cepo cambiario, limiting individuals' purchases of dollars and giving rise to multiple exchange rates.

- Turkey — the government intervened frequently in the currency market to try to stabilize the Turkish lira, drawing on international reserves and regulatory measures.

- Nigeria — for several years, multiple exchange rates (official and parallel) coexisted, reflecting a shortage of dollars in the economy.

These restrictions often push people to look for alternative ways to access dollars and preserve value.

### Taxation of FX transactions

In Brazil, a notable example of FX friction is the IOF (Imposto sobre Operações Financeiras, a tax on financial transactions), which applies to a range of international transactions.

Depending on the nature of the transaction, the tax may be levied on:

- international credit card purchases
- international transfers
- FX transactions carried out by financial institutions

In practice, the final cost to the user may include:

- IOF
- the FX spread applied by the bank
- international transfer fees

Taken together, these elements can make international transactions significantly more expensive, especially for small businesses and individual users.

### Unequal access to the global financial system

Access to the international financial system remains heavily concentrated in traditional banks. To participate fully in that system, individuals and businesses generally need to:

- hold bank accounts at authorized institutions
- meet regulatory and compliance requirements
- navigate bureaucratic processes for international transfers

This creates additional barriers for underbanked populations and for people living in countries less integrated into the global financial system.

## Stablecoin regulation in the United States: GENIUS Act and CLARITY Act

As stablecoins have grown rapidly, regulators have begun debating how to integrate these assets into the traditional financial system. In the United States, two recent legislative initiatives of the Trump administration have taken center stage: the GENIUS Act and the CLARITY Act.

### GENIUS Act

The Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act (GENIUS Act) is the first comprehensive federal legislation aimed at regulating stablecoins in the United States. The law was passed by Congress and signed in July 2025.

Its main goal is to create a clear regulatory framework for stablecoins used as a means of payment.

Key provisions of the law include:

- a requirement for full (1:1) backing in dollars or safe liquid assets
- the option of holding reserves in dollars or U.S. Treasury securities
- periodic disclosure of reserves
- regulatory supervision of issuers
- compliance with AML and anti-money-laundering rules

The legislation also allows banks and financial institutions to issue stablecoins through subsidiaries, bringing these assets into traditional financial infrastructure.

The aim is to build confidence in the sector and strengthen the role of the digital dollar in the global financial system. At the same time, analysts point out that Treasury-backed stablecoins could increase demand for U.S. government debt, which is extremely advantageous for a U.S. government carrying more than 30 trillion dollars in public debt.

### CLARITY Act

While the GENIUS Act focuses specifically on stablecoins, the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act seeks to establish a broader regulatory framework for the digital asset market.

The bill was passed by the House of Representatives in 2025 and aims to settle a long-running regulatory turf war between two U.S. agencies:

- SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission)
- CFTC (Commodity Futures Trading Commission)

The legislation creates specific categories for digital assets, including:

- digital commodities
- investment contract assets
- payment stablecoins

Based on this classification, the bill determines which agency will have regulatory authority over each type of asset.

The central goal is to replace the model known as “regulation by enforcement,” in which companies face regulatory action without clear rules, with a system of defined criteria for how the crypto market operates.

## Implication: a system with structural frictions

Taken together, these factors show that the international FX system still has significant structural frictions.

The main challenges include:

- high intermediation costs
- long settlement times
- taxes and FX spreads
- regulatory restrictions on access to foreign currencies

In this context, stablecoins are emerging as a technological alternative capable of reducing some of these frictions, enabling the direct transfer of dollar-pegged digital value over public blockchain networks.

At the same time, the growing adoption of these assets has fueled regulatory debates in many jurisdictions. In the United States, initiatives such as the GENIUS Act and the CLARITY Act point to a move toward the institutionalization of stablecoins and a gradual integration of blockchain infrastructure with the global financial system.
