In 2024, stablecoin transaction volume topped trillions of dollars, rivaling the major traditional payment networks. At the same time, billions of dollars move every day through financial protocols that run without banks, without business hours and without borders. All of this points to a new financial infrastructure that is already taking shape, even if most people have barely noticed.

For decades, the global financial system has rested on a simple principle: institutional trust. We trust banks to keep accurate records, governments to guarantee the validity of money and intermediaries to execute transactions properly. That model made it possible to build a complex global economy, but it also created structural dependencies that have yet to be resolved.

Blockchain emerges as an alternative to that model. Instead of concentrating control over and validation of transactions in institutions, it spreads that responsibility across the network's participants. Trust no longer sits with specific entities; it is upheld by cryptography, distributed consensus and open mathematical rules.

## What blockchain actually is

Whenever a transaction takes place in the traditional system, there is always an entity responsible for recording and validating it. That record lives in internal systems that the institution controls and updates.

Blockchain proposes an entirely different logic. Instead of a single central system recording everything, there is a ledger shared among thousands of participants. Each participant keeps a copy of that record and follows the updates made to the network in real time.

That shared ledger is the blockchain itself. It works as a collective record of transactions, in which participants continuously agree on what happened and in what order. That agreement does not depend on a central authority, but on technical rules and distributed validation.

## Blockchain from a technical standpoint

Technically speaking, a blockchain is a distributed digital ledger in which transactions are recorded in sequence and shared among all participants in the network. The system is maintained by nodes, which store the full history and validate new operations.

When a transaction is created, it is broadcast to the network and propagated among the nodes, waiting temporarily in a queue until it is included in a block. On networks like Bitcoin, this is done by miners under the Proof of Work consensus model, while networks like Ethereum use Proof of Stake, in which validators are selected based on their economic stake.

Whatever the model, new blocks are added continuously and cryptographically linked to the previous ones. This ensures the history is shared, verifiable and extremely hard to tamper with, allowing the network to operate without relying on a central authority.

## What blockchain really makes possible

Blockchain's main innovation lies not just in the technology, but in the way it lets us organize economic relationships. It enables direct transactions between participants, with no intermediaries.

That makes faster, more efficient global payments possible, along with programmable contracts, known as smart contracts, which are widely used on networks such as Ethereum. Another major advance is asset tokenization, which makes it possible to represent real-world assets, such as real estate, in digital form, broadening access to investment and boosting liquidity.

On top of that, decentralized financial systems (DeFi) are emerging, in which protocols such as Uniswap and Aave offer financial services, like collateralized lending and crypto-asset trading, without the need for traditional institutions.

## Why this beats the traditional system

Today's financial system has structural limitations. International transfers are slow and expensive, settlement can take days, and the presence of multiple intermediaries adds cost, friction and operational complexity.

Blockchain cuts these inefficiencies by enabling direct transactions, faster settlement and less reliance on third parties. A transparent ledger and open access make the system more auditable, more programmable and potentially more inclusive. Beyond making old processes more efficient, it also opens the door to new market formats and new kinds of assets that used to be too difficult or too expensive to exist.

One of the most important aspects is precisely the programmability of money and assets. It becomes possible to create financial instruments with automated rules for issuance, trading, settlement and distribution of returns.

That ranges from tokenizing real estate, receivables, private equity and debt securities to newer structures such as tokenized futures, fractional ownership of artwork, music royalties, assets tied to supply chains, agricultural commodities and even rights to future revenue streams.

In practice, blockchain doesn't just improve existing financial infrastructure; it expands what can be turned into an asset, traded digitally and settled almost instantly. That means thousands of new possibilities for different financial products and far greater efficiency.

## Where this is already happening today

Blockchain is already being used in a range of contexts. Bitcoin works as a global decentralized monetary system and an excellent store of value. Stablecoins such as USDC and USDT are widely used for payments and transfers of value.

In the DeFi ecosystem, billions of dollars flow through open financial protocols. Companies also use blockchain for international payments and asset settlement.

Initiatives connecting the traditional system (TradFi) with the decentralized world are also starting to appear. One example is Rayls, which aims to integrate banks and financial institutions into blockchain-based infrastructure, allowing traditional assets to be tokenized and to operate more efficiently and with KYC (Know Your Customer).

Other initiatives include projects by large financial institutions exploring tokenization, digital custody and on-chain infrastructure, signaling a clear move toward convergence between TradFi and DeFi.

## The real problems and challenges

Despite its progress, blockchain still faces significant challenges. Scalability limits transaction throughput on some networks, and the user experience remains complex. Cryptocurrency volatility gets in the way of everyday use, and self-custody carries risks, since losing private keys means irreversibly losing access.

Regulation is still taking shape, which creates uncertainty, and models like Bitcoin's face criticism over energy consumption. These factors show that the technology is still evolving.

## Where this is heading

The trend points toward a hybrid model. Traditional financial institutions are already exploring blockchain for asset tokenization, settlement and new financial products.

At the same time, the decentralized ecosystem keeps evolving, creating alternatives to the traditional system. The future is likely to be a convergence of TradFi and DeFi, with shared infrastructure and new operating models.
