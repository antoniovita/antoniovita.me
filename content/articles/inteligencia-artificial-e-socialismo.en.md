Over the past few decades, and especially in the last five years, we have witnessed extraordinary advances in computing power and the development of artificial intelligence systems. Currently, algorithms can analyse enormous volumes of data, identify patterns of behaviour, predict demand and optimise processes that, until recently, would have been virtually impossible to manage manually. Given this scenario, one question seems particularly interesting to me: if we are developing machines that are increasingly capable of processing information and making decisions, why not use them to organise the entire global economy?

Imagine, for example, an artificial intelligence that had access to the inventories of all companies, the production capacity of each industry, the available natural resources, and the consumption habits of millions of people. In theory, we could feed it with this information and allow it to determine how much to produce, which resources to use, and how to distribute the products among the population. There would be no need to rely on numerous business owners making decisions individually, competing with each other, and eventually wasting resources on investments that do not work out. Everything could be coordinated by a central system, guided by mathematical models and sophisticated algorithms.

This possibility leads us to revisit an economic debate that began more than a century ago and remains extremely relevant: would it be possible to rationally organise an economy without a free market? More specifically, could artificial intelligence finally make a communist economy based on centralised production planning viable?

To understand why I believe the answer remains and will always be no, we need to understand something I consider one of the most fascinating features of a market economy: price is not simply a number assigned to a commodity, but a way of transmitting information.

## Price as information

When we enter a supermarket and find a particular product being sold at a higher price than usual, our first reaction is usually to think about how much that will affect our budget. We rarely stop to reflect on the amount of economic information that is being conveyed by that number, or on the complexity of the events that contributed to its formation.

Imagine a major coffee-producing region facing a prolonged drought, which significantly impacts its harvest. With a smaller quantity of coffee available to meet demand, it is natural that its price will face upward pressure, assuming all other conditions remain constant. This increase does not only affect the farmers directly involved in production, but also traders, distributors, companies that use coffee as a raw material, and consumers around the world.

The interesting thing is that none of these people necessarily need to know that there was a drought, what its intensity was, or how many hectares of crops were affected. A consumer can simply decide to reduce their consumption in the face of higher prices, while a coffee shop can look for alternative suppliers, adjust its menu, or reconsider the amount of coffee it keeps in stock. At the same time, producers in other regions begin to find economic incentives to expand their production, as the new price makes this decision profitable.

All these decisions happen in a decentralised manner, without any authority being responsible for informing each participant in the economy individually about what they should do. The increase in price communicates that that resource has become relatively scarcer in the face of existing demand, encouraging millions of people to reconsider their choices.

This does not mean that prices are perfect or that they reflect the entire economic reality. They can also be influenced by taxes, regulations, misguided expectations, market power and numerous other factors. Their importance lies precisely in the fact that they condense information about supply conditions, demand and individual assessments into signals that can guide decisions without requiring each person to know the entire chain of events behind them.

It was this characteristic that Friedrich Hayek explored in his essay The Use of Knowledge in Society, published in 1945. For the Austrian economist, one of the main problems faced by any economic organisation is that the knowledge needed to use resources efficiently is not concentrated in a single person or institution, not even in an AI, however well trained it may be, but is dispersed among millions of individuals, each possessing specific information about their circumstances, needs and possibilities.

The price system allows some of this dispersed knowledge to be communicated and used by other individuals, even when they are completely unaware of its origin. In other words, we do not need to know all the events that caused a change in economic conditions in order to respond to the incentives produced by it.

This is one of the reasons why I find the free market so interesting: there is an extraordinarily complex coordination taking place continuously, without anyone needing to know or control all the decisions that make it up.

## The problem of economic calculation

Long before Hayek published his essay, another Austrian economist, Ludwig von Mises, had already presented a fundamental critique of central planning. In 1920, in the work Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth, Mises argued that a socialist economy that eliminated private ownership of the means of production would face a problem related to the very possibility of carrying out rational economic calculations.

To understand his argument, we can imagine an economic authority needing to decide between building a railway or expanding a highway. Both alternatives require scarce resources, such as steel, concrete, machinery, energy and labour, which could be used in various other projects. The question is not simply whether there is enough material to carry out one of the projects, but rather to determine which alternative represents the most economically appropriate use of these resources, taking into account all the other possibilities that will need to be sacrificed.

In a market economy, the prices of capital goods, formed through exchanges between owners, allow for the comparison of costs and expected revenues, the evaluation of investments, and the consideration of the opportunity costs involved in each decision. Obviously, this does not mean that entrepreneurs always make the right decisions, but that they have monetary references to evaluate alternatives and that their mistakes can be revealed by losses.

According to Mises, when the means of production are no longer freely traded, the formation of genuine market prices for these resources disappears. A central authority may know the technical characteristics of each material, its available quantity, and even all the physically possible combinations for its use, but it will still face difficulties in determining which alternatives are economically most advantageous without the prices produced by free exchanges.

It is important to understand that this argument does not depend on a supposed intellectual incapacity of the planners, nor on the absence of sufficiently powerful LLMs. The problem lies in the origin of the information used to perform the calculations. After all, artificial intelligence can perform operations with extraordinary speed, but the sophistication of its calculations does not automatically solve the absence of the economically relevant information that should feed them.

## Wouldn’t artificial intelligence solve this problem?

This is precisely where the discussion becomes more interesting, especially for those, like me, who study Computer Science and follow the development of artificial intelligence.

It would be unfair to ignore the fact that technology has profoundly changed some of the practical difficulties faced by economic planning systems. Today, we can monitor production chains in real time, develop demand forecasting models, optimise logistics routes and manage inventories much more efficiently than would have been possible in the first half of the 20th century. There are even contemporary proposals for computational economic planning that seek to use these capabilities to overcome difficulties traditionally associated with socialism.

Meanwhile, I believe there is an important difference between improving our ability to process information and replacing the mechanisms responsible for producing it.

An artificial intelligence can analyse consumption data to predict how many mobile phones will be sold next year, but how could it possibly know in advance about all the innovations that are yet to be developed, the preferences that will change, and the business opportunities that no one has yet discovered? How could it assess the opportunity cost of the unpredictable happening? In other words, how can we determine the economic value of a product that does not yet exist, or know which ideas deserve investment before they are actually put to the test?

The market does not function merely as a mechanism for the distribution of known resources, but also as a continuous process of discovery. Entrepreneurs formulate hypotheses about what consumers want, invest their own or others’ resources, and put these hypotheses to the test. Some initiatives prove to be economically viable, others fail, and this process continuously modifies the information available to all participants. For this reason, the free market is such a positive system and, in a way, intrinsic to human nature. The market is capable of transmitting information on a global scale through the price system, without depending on the coordination of a central authority. Millions of individual decisions ultimately contribute to a continuous process of adaptation and economic progress. It is a phenomenon that resembles Darwinian evolution: different ideas and strategies are constantly put to the test, while those that best respond to the needs of individuals tend to thrive, in a gradual process of trial, error, and improvement.

An artificial intelligence could participate in this process, helping businesspeople to discover opportunities and make better predictions. It could also simulate scenarios, establish internal benchmark prices, and coordinate extremely complex production systems. Nevertheless, these capabilities do not demonstrate that it would be possible to completely replace price formation through voluntary exchanges, especially when we consider that the information relevant to economic activity is constantly changing.

We could, of course, imagine an artificial system capable of simulating markets and using calculated prices to coordinate production. But this would lead us to another question: where would the information used to determine these prices come from, and how would the system discover whether its assessments correspond to the actual preferences and opportunity costs of individuals?

If prices were determined by a central algorithm, rather than emerging from actual buying, selling, and investment decisions, we would need to demonstrate that this algorithm could adequately reproduce the process by which prices are formed. It would not be enough to show that it can solve a mathematical optimization problem, because the challenge also lies in identifying which data, preferences, and objectives should guide this calculation.

For this reason, I do not believe that the advances in artificial intelligence render the arguments presented by Mises and Hayek obsolete. On the contrary, they offer an interesting opportunity to understand the difference between centralising processing power and centralising economic knowledge.

## An economy is more than just an optimisation problem

There is still one aspect of this discussion that I consider particularly relevant. In computer science, we are used to defining problems, setting objectives and developing algorithms capable of finding solutions that satisfy certain criteria. However, when we apply this logic to an entire economy, we encounter an additional difficulty: there is not necessarily a single optimisation function that represents what all individuals desire.

One person may prefer to work less and receive a lower salary, while another may be willing to give up their free time to increase their income. Some consumers value cheaper products, while others prioritise quality, exclusivity, convenience, or aspects that could hardly be reduced to a universal objective measure.

These preferences are not only different, but often incompatible, and they change according to the circumstances of each individual. For an artificial intelligence to manage the entire economy, someone would need to determine how these preferences would be compared, which objectives would be given higher priority, and which sacrifices would be considered acceptable.

The free market does not eliminate conflicts between individual interests, nor does it guarantee that everyone will achieve the desired results. What it does allow is for different people to pursue their own objectives and coordinate part of their actions through voluntary exchanges and prices, without relying on an authority that establishes a single order of priorities for the entire society.

In my view, this decentralisation represents not only an economic advantage, but also an important principle of individual freedom.

## Freedom is not just a matter of efficiency

Up to this point, we have mainly analysed the economic arguments against replacing the market with a system of central planning. However, I believe that there is an equally important dimension to this discussion, especially when we look at the problem from an objectivist perspective.

Even if it were possible to develop an artificial intelligence capable of managing resources with great efficiency, this would not answer the question of who should have the authority to make decisions on behalf of all individuals.

A society could exhibit satisfactory economic indicators and yet severely restrict the freedom of its citizens to produce, undertake ventures, invest, or use their property according to their own preferences. In this scenario, the technical quality of the decisions made by the central system would not eliminate the ethical issues involved in the concentration of economic power.

The liberal-libertarian defence of the free market, therefore, is not limited to the idea that competition tends to produce economically desirable results. It is also based on the autonomy of individuals and the legitimacy of voluntary relationships, in opposition to the imposition of mandatory economic planning.

It is perfectly possible to advocate for the use of artificial intelligence to assist in economic decision-making, including in companies and public institutions, without concluding that the entire economy should be subordinate to the decisions of a single system.

## Ultimately, could artificial intelligence make communism feasible?

I believe that advances in artificial intelligence will continue to transform the way we produce, consume and manage resources. Companies will be able to make decisions with increasingly accurate information, production chains will become more efficient, and various economic activities will be reorganised by technologies that we are only just beginning to understand.

Meanwhile, when we consider the possibility of using these tools to replace the free market with a centrally planned economy, fundamental questions remain that cannot be resolved simply with greater computing power.

The price, as Austrian economists have demonstrated, is not simply a piece of data that an authority needs to collect in order to carry out its calculations. It is also a result of economic interactions, a signal produced by decentralised decisions that communicates information about relative scarcity, demand and alternative uses of resources.

The absence of these signals cannot be automatically compensated for by the ability to process larger volumes of data. Similarly, the ability to predict behaviours does not equate to knowing in advance all the discoveries, preferences and opportunities that will arise from human actions.

This does not constitute proof that any conceivable form of computational planning is doomed to failure, but it does offer important reasons to question the idea that artificial intelligence, on its own, would have solved the problem of economic calculation or overcome the difficulties identified by Hayek.

Personally, I find it much more interesting to imagine artificial intelligence enhancing the capabilities of millions of individuals and companies than replacing their decisions with a central authority. After all, if technology allows each person to have access to extraordinary tools for analysis, prediction, and creation, why should we assume that its main economic application should be to concentrate all these capabilities in a single planner? That’s why I like the phrase “No truly good idea needs to be imposed.” It sums up well the importance of allowing a plurality of initiatives, rather than just one centralised AI, which, even if it is far superior to any human, still does not have more knowledge than billions of individuals making decisions.

The great contribution of artificial intelligence to economic organisation does not lie in finally making central planning possible, but in expanding the possibilities of a decentralised economy, in which individuals can use tools that are increasingly sophisticated to pursue their own goals.

And, in that sense, I believe that technological progress does not diminish the importance of the free market. It offers us new reasons to understand how information, incentives and individual decisions relate to the formation of an economic order that no participant, individually, needs to fully understand.
