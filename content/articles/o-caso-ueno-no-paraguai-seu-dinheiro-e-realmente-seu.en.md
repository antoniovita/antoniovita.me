The digitization of finance has made it easier to open accounts, transfer funds and invest. With a few taps on a screen, money seems to be available at all times. But the ease of moving a balance can hide a deeper question: how much control do we really have over the funds we entrust to an institution?

The Ueno case in Paraguay brought that question to the center of the debate. The crisis highlighted the importance of the institutional response and opened room to discuss supervision, accountability and financial autonomy. It also put back on the table a question that has followed Bitcoin since its origin: the dependence on intermediaries to move value.

## What happened with Ueno

In a statement published on November 3, 2026, the Central Bank of Paraguay announced the start of the resolution procedure for Ueno, approved by its board the day before. According to the regulator, adjustments to the financial statements showed that the institution's solvency ratio was below 50% of the required minimum. This means the indicator had fallen to less than half of the regulatory floor, not that the bank had to keep half of its deposits in cash. ([Central Bank of Paraguay](https://www.bcp.gov.py/web/institucional/w/banco-central-inicia-el-procedimiento-de-resolucion-de-ueno-bank))

Resolution is a legal procedure for dealing with a financial institution in distress. In the statement, the BCP presented its goals as protecting depositors and preserving the stability of the financial system.

In the authorities' initial explanation, the Deposit Guarantee Fund covered up to 75 monthly minimum wages per depositor, per financial institution, equivalent at the time to 228.3 million guaraníes. That protection, however, did not mean immediate availability: customers faced a freeze on operations while the procedure was being organized. ([ABC Color](https://www.abc.com.py/economia/2026/11/03/ueno-bank-bcp-explica-la-resolucion-y-que-va-a-pasar-ahora/))

The difference between having protected funds and being able to use them is decisive. For a family that has bills to pay or a business that depends on working capital, time without access to funds is also a cost.

## The merit of acting and the duty to explain

The decision to confront the deterioration of a financial institution deserves recognition. Preserving assets, organizing the handling of deposits and limiting the spread of the crisis are essential tasks.

The BCP announced it was sending approximately 120 staff members to Ueno's branches to reconcile assets and liabilities. The mobilization is a concrete response measure, although its outcome should be judged by how the process is carried out. ([ABC Color](https://www.abc.com.py/economia/2026/11/03/ueno-bank-bcp-explica-la-resolucion-y-que-va-a-pasar-ahora/))

That recognition must go hand in hand with a demand for explanations. When were the problems identified? What measures were required? What information was available to customers?

An intervention can be necessary and well run while the earlier supervision remains open to question. The institutional assessment must cover both stages. The merit of managing an emergency does not close the discussion about what could have been done to prevent it.

## The balance on the screen and control over the money

A bank account offers valuable services: safekeeping of funds, payments, transfers and access to credit. The balance shown in the app represents a claim the customer holds against the institution. Using it depends on the bank continuing to operate and on the applicable rules.

This relationship usually goes unnoticed when everything works. An instant transfer gives a sense of direct control. A crisis reveals the conditions on which that access depends.

This is where the debate approaches Bitcoin. Its original paper, published in 2008, proposes a system of electronic payments between participants, with no need for an intermediary financial institution. Transactions are validated according to rules shared by the network. ([Satoshi Nakamoto](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf))

The question then becomes which functions must be handed over to third parties and which can remain under the user's control.

## Deposits, credit and Jesús Huerta de Soto's critique

The difference between seeing a balance and having the funds immediately available holds a central place in the work of Jesús Huerta de Soto. In *Dinero, crédito bancario y ciclos económicos* (Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles), the economist criticizes the fractional-reserve system, in which banks keep only a portion of demand deposits in reserve and use the rest in lending operations.

Huerta de Soto advocates a 100% reserve requirement for demand deposits and a separation between the safekeeping of money available for withdrawal and funds earmarked for loans. In his interpretation, this distinction is essential to reconcile the commitments banks take on with the availability promised to depositors. ([Huerta de Soto](https://www.jesushuertadesoto.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Libro-completo.-DINERO-CREDITO-BANCARIO.pdf))

This perspective offers a lens for discussing the Ueno case, although it does not by itself establish the causes of the crisis. Insufficient capital, lack of liquidity and fractional reserves are different concepts. Investigating the episode requires looking at the institution's specific assets, operations and decisions.

The question the author raises, however, remains relevant: does the customer understand the difference between handing over money for safekeeping and financing an intermediation business?

This is also where the connection to crypto assets arises. Self-custody lets users control the keys that authorize the movement of their assets, changing their relationship with intermediaries. This is not equivalent to Huerta de Soto's proposal for banking reform, but it brings the two debates together around one concern: making the relationship between ownership, availability and responsibility clearer.

## What self-custody changes

With self-custody, users manage the keys that authorize transactions with their crypto assets. This reduces dependence on a custodian to move them.

That control comes with concrete responsibilities. Losing the keys, or the information needed to recover the wallet, can permanently block access to the assets. Ethereum's documentation stresses the need to protect the recovery phrase and keep it out of anyone else's reach. ([Ethereum.org](https://ethereum.org/wallets/))

It is also necessary to distinguish self-custody from keeping crypto assets on a platform. When a company controls the keys, users still depend on an intermediary to access and withdraw their funds.

The choice involves different distributions of responsibility. Third-party custody requires trust in the service provider. Self-custody requires the ability to protect your own credentials. Neither eliminates market risk or guarantees that an asset will hold its value.

The Ueno case, therefore, does not show that any crypto alternative is superior to a bank account. It offers an opportunity to compare models more clearly: who can move the funds, what information can the user verify, and what happens when one party fails?

## Freedom of choice requires information

Individual responsibility depends on real conditions for choice. A depositor does not necessarily have the knowledge or the documents needed to replicate a bank examination. Likewise, anyone using a digital wallet needs to understand the consequences of managing their own keys.

Transparency, education and competition make these choices more sound. The ability to use different services and forms of custody expands citizens' autonomy, provided the risks and commitments of each option are understandable.

Those who manage other people's funds must also answer for their decisions. The power to take risks should come with responsibility for the consequences. This principle applies to bank managers, crypto platform operators and the authorities responsible for oversight, within the remit of each.

Recognizing the work of Paraguay's institutions in managing the crisis is compatible with demanding clear contracts, verifiable information and accountability.

## Trust after Ueno

The conduct of Paraguay's institutions should be recognized for its concrete measures and assessed by its results: the treatment of depositors, recovery of assets, public communication and accountability.

The same rigor should extend to prevention. A complete institutional response needs to explain how the situation developed and which changes can reduce the chance of a repeat.

The crypto world's contribution to this discussion lies in making the relationship between ownership, custody and control more visible. Having a balance, being able to move it and directly controlling its credentials are different situations.

The Ueno case leaves a question that goes beyond Paraguay: when we say the money is ours, do we understand whom we depend on to use it?
